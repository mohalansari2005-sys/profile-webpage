## What the work actually involved

Majara is a Saudi startup builder that turn business ideas into products and scalable businesses. I joined the team as a Product Engineering Intern, working hybrid out of Riyadh. Because the team is small and the work is closely tied to building new products, the role has been broader than a typical internship focused on a predefined engineering backlog.

My work has primarily involved building backend services and REST APIs in Python, connecting those services to frontend applications and external integrations, and prototyping new product capabilities from an initial requirement through to working software. The work often starts before there is a fully defined technical specification, which means part of the job is understanding the intended workflow, identifying what needs to exist technically, and then deciding how to structure and implement it.

A significant part of the role has been working directly with stakeholders to turn business needs into something an engineering team can actually build. I've participated in stakeholder discussions to understand the problem, clarify workflows, challenge ambiguous requirements, and translate those conversations into MVP definitions, functional requirements, user roles, and acceptance criteria. My previous experience in business development at SEET helped significantly here: I'm already comfortable speaking with clients, asking questions to uncover the underlying need, and translating business conversations into a concrete solution. At Majara, I've been able to apply that same skill from the engineering side — taking what a stakeholder needs and translating it into something that can actually be designed and built.

Once the requirements were defined, I worked on translating them into the technical structure behind the product: data models, API contracts, business logic, and system workflows. I then implemented and iterated on those capabilities as requirements became clearer through development. This exposed me to a part of product engineering that isn't visible in the final codebase: deciding what belongs in the MVP, identifying missing edge cases, and recognizing when a requirement needs to be clarified before it becomes an implementation problem.

The backend work has included Python, FastAPI, SQLAlchemy, PostgreSQL, Redis, Celery, Docker, and REST API design. I've also worked with external APIs and asynchronous workflows, which introduced practical engineering concerns around rate limits, retries, failures, and keeping the system predictable when dependencies don't behave perfectly.

I've also gained experience on the frontend side, working with React to connect product interfaces to the backend services I was building. This gave me a better understanding of the full path from user interaction to API request, business logic, database operation, and response back to the interface. Working across both sides also made it easier to spot gaps between what an API technically exposes and what the actual product experience requires.

Because the products are B2B, the users and constraints are different from those of a typical consumer application. The software needs to fit into an existing company's processes rather than simply provide a standalone feature. Multi-tenancy, access boundaries, predictable behavior, data integrity, and the ability to support different organizational workflows therefore become important parts of the design from the beginning.

Outside of my direct product work with Majara, I've also been deliberately developing my knowledge of AI engineering and LLM-based systems through personal projects and experimentation. That has included exploring tools such as LangChain and LangGraph and learning how retrieval, agent orchestration, and LLMs can be incorporated into software systems. These are currently areas I'm building toward rather than claiming as production AI experience.

What has made the experience particularly valuable is the exposure to the full path from **problem → stakeholder needs → MVP definition → requirements → architecture → implementation → iteration**. Rather than receiving a finished specification and only being responsible for the code, I've had to develop the ability to understand what is being asked, question gaps in the requirements, make engineering decisions, and turn an ambiguous idea into working software.
