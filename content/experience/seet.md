## What the work actually involved

Before Majara, I spent a few months in business development at SEET (صيت), a marketing solutions agency in Riyadh. The role was almost the inverse of engineering work: instead of starting with a technical requirement and figuring out how to implement it, I started with a client, their business objective, and often a loosely defined problem, then worked backward to determine what solution actually made sense.

The work was primarily client-facing and involved understanding what organizations were trying to achieve, identifying where SEET's services could create value, and turning those conversations into concrete proposals. Day to day, that meant participating in client meetings, understanding requirements and pain points, preparing sales pitches and proposal decks, presenting solutions, and staying involved in the relationship after the initial proposal rather than treating a signed deal as the end of the interaction.

A particularly useful part of the role was learning that clients rarely describe their problems in a way that maps neatly onto a service or solution. A client might come in with a broad objective, a business challenge, or simply an idea of what they think they need. The job was to ask the right questions, understand the underlying objective, and translate that ambiguity into a solution that was both useful to the client and realistically deliverable by the agency.

That translation happened at several levels: understanding the client's business problem, determining what SEET could realistically offer, communicating the value of that solution, and presenting it in a way that made sense to someone who wasn't necessarily interested in the underlying execution details.

The experience also taught me that business development isn't simply about selling. A large part of it is managing expectations and building enough understanding between two sides that the proposed solution actually matches the problem. A technically impressive solution that solves the wrong problem is still a bad solution, just as a persuasive pitch for something the client doesn't actually need is ultimately a bad outcome.

I think about this experience often on the engineering side now. A badly scoped technical requirement and a badly scoped sales pitch can fail for essentially the same reason: the person defining the solution didn't fully understand the problem. The skill that fixes both is the same one I developed at SEET — sit with the client or stakeholder, ask questions, challenge assumptions when necessary, and understand what they actually need rather than simply building around what they first asked for.

That experience is one of the reasons I don't see engineering and business development as completely separate skill sets. Engineering taught me how to build systems; SEET taught me to spend more time understanding **what should actually be built in the first place**.
